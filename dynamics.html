Dynamics Systems Response Lab

Status: Complete

Project Overview

Welcome to the showcase of my Dynamics Systems Response Lab[cite: 4]. In this project, I measured and analyzed the response of a dynamical system over time[cite: 4]. The primary goal was to utilize raw data to estimate material characteristics, such as spring constants and damping coefficients, and to derive the governing equations of motion for the system[cite: 4]. The experiment tested four main configurations—comparing linear versus non-linear responses and zero-block versus four-block masses—along with one supplementary non-linear configuration[cite: 4].

Testing Setup & Methodology

Data was gathered using a combination of manual component measurements and computer-aided data acquisition[cite: 4]. The physical setup was carefully constructed to minimize external errors:

  • The experimental setup utilized a 1-DOF sliding cart system equipped with an embedded linear optical shaft encoder[cite: 4].
  • The optical encoder measured instantaneous displacement by sensing alternating light intensities via LEDs and photodetectors[cite: 4].
  • The sliding mechanism was oriented 45 degrees from the edge of the table to minimize the impact of external forces or accidental bumps[cite: 4].
  • Individual component masses were measured with a digital scale, and the cart was hung vertically to ensure its mass was isolated from the broader sliding mechanism[cite: 4].

Linear Data Analysis & Modeling

For the linear system response, the initial spring constant ($k_{1}$) and damping coefficient ($b$) were estimated using simple harmonic and damped oscillation principles[cite: 4]. These values were later refined using trial and error in MATLAB until the analytical model overlapped with the raw experimental data[cite: 4].

  • The time period of oscillation was used to estimate the spring constant via the equation $k_{1}=m(\frac{2\pi}{T})^{2}$[cite: 4].
  • The damping coefficient was estimated by comparing successive peak displacements using $b=\frac{2m}{T}ln(\frac{x_{1}}{x_{2}})$[cite: 4].
  • The linear equation of motion was derived and represented in the form $m\dot{x}+b\dot{x}+k_{1}x=0$[cite: 4].

Non-Linear System Analysis

The non-linear configurations introduced a second spring (Spring 4) acting as a damper, which disproportionately absorbed kinetic energy and only engaged after the cart passed a specific offset distance[cite: 4].

  • Because Spring 4 did not act on the cart for the entire period of oscillation, a Heaviside function, $H(x-x_{c})$, was implemented to mathematically model the step-like engagement of the second spring[cite: 4].
  • The non-linear equation of motion was formulated as $m\dot{x}+b\dot{x}+k_{1}x+k_{2}(x-x_{c})H(x-x_{c})=0$[cite: 4].
  • The effective spring stiffness of the non-linear system dynamically transitioned from $k_{1}$ to $k_{1}+k_{2}$ depending on whether the cart was in contact with the damper[cite: 4].

Key Findings & Results

Through experimental testing and iterative MATLAB adjustments, the actual system parameters for the dynamics models were successfully identified[cite: 4].

  • The final spring constant for Spring 1 ($k_{1}$) was determined to be 81.77 N/m[cite: 4].
  • The actual damping coefficient ($b$) for the system was calculated to be 0.1668 Ns/m[cite: 4].
  • The second spring constant ($k_{2}$) in the non-linear configuration was 253.0 N/m, with an operational offset distance of 0.0244 m[cite: 4].
  • The total mass of the zero-blocks configuration was 660.11 g, while the addition of the four weight blocks increased the total system mass to 1853.48 g[cite: 4].
